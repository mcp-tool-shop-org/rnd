# Research behind the critic-signal design (2026-10-19)

Three research agents (Sonnet) each answered one question with primary sources, read as full text where a number
is used. These are R&D's summaries of their reports, in R&D's words. Claims start `[unverified]` unless marked.
Numbers come from the papers' tables as the agents read them; none was re-checked by a second reader, since no
decision rests on one figure alone (Standing Rule 3).

## 1. How studies measure an LLM judge's leniency

- **Zheng et al. 2023**, "Judging LLM-as-a-Judge with MT-Bench and Chatbot Arena", https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.05685.
  Judges were order-sensitive (GPT-4 position-consistent 65% of the time) and fooled by padding; self-preference
  appeared but the authors call the data thin.
- **Wang et al. 2023**, "Large Language Models are not Fair Evaluators", https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.17926. Swapping
  answer order flipped many verdicts. The rate at which two orderings disagree needs no human labels.
- **Thakur et al. 2024**, "Judging the Judges", https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.12624. On TriviaQA, most open judges
  called mismatched answers correct 0.87–0.99 of the time. Percent agreement above 90% still hid gaps over 10
  points; a chance-corrected statistic separated the judges.
- **Doddapaneni et al. 2024**, "Finding Blind Spots in Evaluator LLMs with Interpretable Checklists",
  https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.13439. Over 2,400 corrupted real answers across 22 error types, evaluators missed the
  drop in over half on average; a reference cut factual misses sharply.
- **Tyen et al. 2023**, "LLMs cannot find reasoning errors, but can correct them given the error location",
  https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.08516. Asked directly, the strongest model tested found the mistake in 40% of flawed
  traces; a weaker one tended to call every trace clean.
- **Panickssery et al. 2024**, "LLM Evaluators Recognize and Favor Their Own Generations",
  https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.13076. Self-recognition and self-preference rose together.
- Transfer risk: older or closed models, chat and QA tasks; none tests ~30B open models on grounded verdict rows.

## 2. Protocol changes that make a critic stricter

- **Lightman et al. 2023**, "Let's Verify Step by Step", https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.20050. Step-level supervision
  beat outcome supervision on MATH. Trained reward models, so only the decomposition idea transfers.
- **Zheng et al. 2023** (as above): with a reference answer, wrong maths answers accepted fell from 14 of 20 to 3
  of 20. A tiny test.
- **Weng et al. 2023**, "Large Language Models are Better Reasoners with Self-Verification",
  https://arxiv.org/abs/2212.09561. Re-derivation helped arithmetic far more than commonsense.
- **TICK 2024**, "Generated Checklists Improve LLM-based Evaluation and Generation", https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.03608.
  Checklists raised a judge's agreement with human preferences moderately.
- **Min et al. 2023**, "FActScore", https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.14251. Atomic claims checked against evidence
  estimated human scores closely; the checker's strength mattered.
- **Gou et al. 2023**, "CRITIC", https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.11738. Tool-backed critique helped; critique without
  tools gained about nothing on QA and sometimes hurt.
- **Stechly et al. 2024**, "On the Self-Verification Limitations of LLMs on Reasoning and Planning Tasks",
  https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.08115. LLM verifiers traded low false positives for false negatives up to 97% on some
  tasks; a sound external verifier helped a lot.
- Not covered by the agent: graded or probability scoring, pairwise comparison with an exemplar, adversarial
  framing, any-refuse aggregation. Those are untested here.

## 3. How many gold checks, and how to compare protocols

- **Rule of three** (Hanley & Lippman-Hand 1983, JAMA, https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.1983.03330370053031): with no
  failures in n, the 95% upper bound is about 3/n. Exact: 1 − 0.05^(1/n). n = 59 bounds a rate at 5%, n = 29 at 10%.
  `[verified]` (via: R&D recomputed, 2026-10-19).
- **Clopper & Pearson 1934**, Biometrika 26(4), https://doi.org/10.1093/biomet/26.4.404: exact binomial intervals.
  With 1, 2 or 3 failures in 100, the one-sided upper limits are about 4.7%, 6.2% and 7.6%.
- **Paired comparison:** exact McNemar on the discordant pairs. The sample-size formula cited is Connor 1987,
  Biometrics 43(4); the agent quoted it from memory. `[unverified]` A drop from 10% to 3% needs about 126–238
  paired items depending on the discordance.
- **Error seeding** (Mills 1972, IBM technical report, from memory): seeded defects tend to be easier to find than
  natural ones, so a seeded miss rate is a floor. `[unverified]`
- **Two-phase sampling** (Neyman 1938, JASA): worth it only if a cheap signal predicts errors and can split the
  accepted pool. `[unverified: DOI not confirmed]`
